    Gear

    Enter what you paid, the year you bought it, and the year that model first came out. Bought new? Both years are the same. Bought used? The app figures out what it sold for new from your price, then follows the age of the model from there. It shows what it's worth today, how much it loses each year, and the slice each shoot has to earn back.

    These are averages of how used photo and computer gear typically resells, not quotes. Lenses hold value best; Apple computers hold value far better than other computers; camera bodies sit in between and wear faster the more you shoot. Props and other small gear do not have a model year, so enter the year you bought them in both places. If you don't know a model year, use the year you bought it; the estimate will run a little high, which is the safe direction.

    Bills and one-time purchases

    Everything is turned into a monthly amount, then split across your shoots per month.

    Monthly: Adobe Lightroom + Photoshop, website, cloud backup, insurance. Yearly: domain name, business license. One-time: a course, a preset pack, software you bought outright. One-time items are spread over the years you expect to get value from them, usually 2 to 3 for a course or software.

    Travel

    The IRS standard mileage rate covers gas, wear, insurance, and depreciation together. It was 70 cents for 2025; check the current year's rate and update this once a year.

    Add-ons and cushion

    Flat amount added to the total when the extended family box is checked on a session.

    Added on top of costs and pay. It covers self-employment tax (roughly 15%), income tax, reshoots, no-shows, client emails, and saving toward the next lens. 30 to 40 percent is a normal starting point.
